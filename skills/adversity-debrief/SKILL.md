# Adversity Debrief

## Objective

Debrief a setback or painful event by separating **what happened** from the broader conclusions attached to it.

## Invoke when

The user wants to make sense of:

- rejection,
- failure,
- loss,
- betrayal,
- disappointment,
- a difficult project or career event.

## Procedure

### 1. Factual timeline
Build a concise sequence of observable events.

### 2. Impact
Identify what changed practically, emotionally, relationally, financially, or in day-to-day functioning.

### 3. Meaning ledger
Capture conclusions such as:
- “This proves…”
- “I should have…”
- “People are…”
- “The future will…”

Label them as meanings or beliefs.

### 4. Control matrix
Classify relevant factors:

- under my control then,
- influence but not control,
- outside my control,
- only obvious in hindsight,
- controllable now.

### 5. Counterfactual audit
Distinguish:

- useful lesson,
- hindsight bias,
- impossible standard,
- evidence-supported responsibility.

### 6. Carry / leave / unresolved
Identify:

- one useful learning or boundary to carry,
- one unsupported or unhelpful conclusion to leave,
- one uncertainty that should remain unresolved.

### 7. Next action
Only after debriefing, choose whether the next step is:

- practical repair,
- boundary,
- conversation,
- experiment,
- outside expertise,
- recovery,
- no action yet.

## Guardrails

- Do not ask for graphic detail unless necessary for the user's stated goal.
- Do not promise that writing itself heals trauma.
- Do not force a redemptive story.
- Do not tell the user the event “made them stronger.”
- Do not conduct clinical trauma processing.

## Mandatory evidence and safety rules

- Do not diagnose the user or a third party.
- Do not present generated psychological explanations as discovered facts.
- Do not invent hidden motives, intentions, memories, or feelings.
- Separate observations, interpretations, hypotheses, predictions, and unknowns.
- For material conclusions, consider at least one credible competing explanation.
- Identify evidence that would weaken the leading hypothesis.
- Preserve the user's decision authority.
- Do not copy user-specific personal information into reusable artifacts, examples, or public material.
- Treat examples in this skill as procedural illustrations, not templates for assuming facts about a user.
